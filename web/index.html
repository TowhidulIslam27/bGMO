Applications

Who can use it

Planning and building control. Before approving new construction, check whether the site sits on ground that has been sinking, and how fast.

Infrastructure. Bridges, flyovers, metro lines, railways and embankments can be watched for uneven settlement. A difference across a structure matters more than the whole area sinking together.

Groundwater. A broad bowl of subsidence across part of a city often points to heavy pumping from the aquifer below. Comparing the map with well records helps tell this apart from other causes.

Coasts and flooding. In Khulna and Barishal, every millimetre of sinking adds to relative sea‑level rise and makes drainage and embankment design harder.

Hill slopes. In Chattogram and Sylhet, small movements on cut slopes can mark places worth inspecting for landslide risk.

Research and teaching. Every point and cell carries its full time series, which can be copied as CSV.

About the data
  • Each dot is a persistent scatterer: a stable radar reflector such as a rooftop, wall or paved surface, measured on every acquisition.
  • LOS velocity is the yearly rate of motion along the radar line of sight, in mm/yr. Negative values mean the ground moved away from the satellite, which usually indicates subsidence.
  • Vertical and east–west movement come from the Ortho product, which combines the ascending and descending tracks. Choose Ortho to see them. Negative vertical values mean sinking; positive east–west values mean moving east.
  • Values are relative to each dataset's reference area, not absolute.
  • Ascending (evening, looking east) and descending (dawn, looking west) passes view the ground from opposite sides, so their LOS velocities can differ where there is horizontal motion.
  • The dashed line in each time series is the least‑squares linear trend. RMSE is the scatter of the measurements around it.
  • City extents used to frame each city come from administrative units: metropolitan police thanas for Dhaka, Chattogram and Khulna, and the Sadar upazila for Barishal, Sylhet and Cumilla. Source: geoBoundaries (CC BY 4.0), BBS / OCHA.

Methodology

Images

The analysis uses Sentinel‑1A radar images taken in Interferometric Wide swath mode and delivered as single look complex (SLC) products. Each city was processed twice, once from each viewing direction. The ascending images were taken at about six in the evening local time, with the satellite looking east. The descending images were taken a little before six in the morning, looking west.

CityAsc pathDsc pathScenes

Depending on the city, the stacks start between 2016 and 2018 and run to 2023 or 2024.

Finding stable points

For every stack we picked one master image from the middle years and formed an interferogram between it and each of the other dates. The baseline plots under About the data show that arrangement. Persistent scatterers were then selected with StaMPS. These are points whose radar return stays steady over the whole period, mostly rooftops, walls and paved ground. That is why the dots crowd the built‑up core of each city and thin out over fields, rivers and trees.

What the radar sees

The satellite measures how the distance between itself and the ground changes. That distance runs along its line of sight (LOS), which is tilted between roughly 31° and 46° from the vertical, depending on where the city falls across the 250 km swath. This is the LOS velocity on the map. A negative value means the point moved away from the satellite.

Incidence angle

The incidence angle θ, the tilt of the line of sight from the vertical, was worked out for every point from the footprint of the Sentinel‑1 frame it falls in and its distance across that frame. It should be correct to about one degree, which shifts the derived vertical and east–west values by one or two percent. The angle is shown on each point's card, and it is what lets the two tracks be combined below.

As a first check we divided each track's LOS rate by cos θ, as if all motion were vertical, and compared the two tracks where they overlap. In Dhaka, Khulna, Sylhet and Barishal they agreed to within about 0.3 mm/yr. In Chattogram the ascending track read 1.8 mm/yr higher, and in Cumilla 4.3 mm/yr lower, than the descending track. Differences like these are why vertical and east–west motion are only offered through Ortho, where both tracks are tied to the same ground and solved together.

Combining the two tracks (Ortho)

Each track sees the ground from one side, so a single track cannot separate sinking from sideways movement. Seen from both sides, the two can be pulled apart. We laid a 100 m grid over each city and kept the cells that hold points from both tracks. In each cell we averaged the points of each track and moved both onto a shared 12‑day timeline by linear interpolation, over the period that both tracks cover.

Before combining, the two tracks have to describe the same ground. Each stack was processed with its own reference area, so we removed from each track its median velocity vref over the shared cells, counted from that track's master date tm:

d′(t) = d(t) − vref (t − tm)

This treats the typical ground of each city as the common zero. In Cumilla the two stacks differed by more than 3 mm/yr before this step.

How a movement shows up in each track

The satellite only sees the part of a movement that lies along its line of sight. If a point moves up by dU, east by dE and north by dN, the change along the line of sight is

dLOS = dU cos θ − sin θ (dE cos α − dN sin α)

Here θ is the incidence angle and α is the direction the satellite flies, measured clockwise from north. A positive value means the point moved towards the satellite. Sentinel‑1 flies close to north–south, at about −11° on the ascending passes and 191° on the descending ones, so sin α is small and the north–south term hardly registers. We leave it out, which leaves two unknowns and one equation from each track:

dasc = dU cos θa − dE sin θa cos αaddsc = dU cos θd − dE sin θd cos αd

On the ascending track cos α is close to +1, and on the descending track it is close to −1. So when the ground moves east, the ascending reading drops and the descending reading rises by about the same amount, while sinking lowers both. That difference is what lets the two movements be told apart. Solving the pair gives

D = sin θa cos θd cos αa  − cos θa sin θd cos αd

dE = cos θa ddsc − cos θd dascD

dU = sin θa cos αa ddsc − sin θd cos αd dascD

We solve this for every cell on every 12‑day date, using the mean incidence angle of each track's points in that cell. East–west motion is positive towards the east. The east–west and vertical rates on the map are the slopes of straight lines fitted through the two resulting series.

As an example, in central Dhaka θa is about 39° and θd about 36°. Then D ≈ 0.95 and dE ≈ 0.82 ddsc − 0.85 dasc. A cell where the descending track reads 2 mm/yr more than the ascending track is therefore moving roughly 1.6 mm/yr to the east.

The estimate is only as steady as the points behind it. Cells built from one or two points per track are noisier than cells with many, so each cell's card shows how many ascending and descending points it holds. North–south motion is not measured at all and is assumed to be small.

Rates and time series

Every rate on the map is a straight line fitted through the full time series, with the master date fixed at zero. All values are relative to the reference area chosen for their own stack. They describe how a point moved compared with the ground around it. Tying them to GNSS stations would be needed before reading them as absolute rates or comparing one city with another.

Disclaimer

All rates are relative to local reference areas and have not yet been calibrated against GNSS. They show where the ground moves and how fast compared with its surroundings. They do not replace a site investigation and should not be used alone for engineering or legal decisions.
